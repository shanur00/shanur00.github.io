Reader Question 2

“Now my equation is $y = mx + b$. Now what is $b$? Error or what?”

$b$ is the y-intercept (also called the bias in machine learning)—not the error. In our linear model $\hat{y} = mx + b$, every symbol has a distinct role:

Intercept / Bias ($b$)
$b \approx 45.714$

The starting value of $\hat{y}$ when $x = 0$. A student who studies 0 hours is predicted to start with a baseline score of ~45.7 points.

Slope / Weight ($m$)
$m \approx 6.369$

How much $\hat{y}$ rises for every 1-hour increase in $x$—about +6.37 exam points per extra hour studied.

Error / Residual ($e$)
$e = y - \hat{y}$

The vertical gap between the actual score ($y$) and the line's predicted score ($\hat{y} = mx + b$).

Section 1.2

Calculating Slope ($m$) and Intercept ($b$) by Hand

Reader Question 4

“Now calculate the $m$ and $b$ first using the formula for our dataset.”

Using the Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) formulas, we find the exact line that minimizes the sum of squared vertical errors across all 8 points:

1. Slope Formula

$$m = \frac{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2}$$

2. Intercept Formula

$$b = \bar{y} - m\bar{x}$$

Step 1: Compute the Sample Means ($\bar{x}$ and $\bar{y}$)

  • $\bar{x} = \frac{1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 6 + 7 + 8}{8} = \frac{36}{8} = \mathbf{4.5}$
  • $\bar{y} = \frac{52 + 58 + 65 + 71 + 78 + 85 + 90 + 96}{8} = \frac{595}{8} = \mathbf{74.375}$

Step 2: Compute Deviations & Sums

2. Intercept ($b$): $\displaystyle b = 74.375 - (6.369 \times 4.5) = 74.375 - 28.6607 \approx \mathbf{45.714}$

Final Regression Equation: $\quad \hat{y} = 6.369x + 45.714$

Section 1.3

Signal vs. Noise ($y = mx + b + \epsilon$)

Reader Question 5

“But we learn about noise. What is that? Show it using visualization.”

In real-world data, points never sit on a razor-sharp mathematical line. Every observation $y$ is a combination of two components:

$$y = \underbrace{mx + b}_{\text{Signal (the pattern)}} + \underbrace{\epsilon}_{\text{Noise (randomness)}}$$
  • The Signal ($mx + b$): The predictable underlying trend—each extra study hour adds roughly $6.37$ points.
  • The Noise ($\epsilon$): All unmeasured real-world factors—how well the student slept, test anxiety, lucky guesses, or tricky wording. For instance, at $x = 2$ hours, the line predicts $58.45$ while the actual score is $58$ ($e = -0.45$); at $x = 6$ hours, the line predicts $83.93$ while the actual score is $85$ ($e = +1.07$).
  • Residual vs. Noise: Because true noise $\epsilon$ is unobservable, we estimate it using the observed vertical gap $e = y - \hat{y}$, called the residual.

Why does $\sum e = 0.00$?

The positive residuals ($+0.18 + 0.44 + 1.07 = +1.69$) and negative residuals ($-0.08 - 0.45 - 0.19 - 0.30 - 0.67 = -1.69$) balance out completely because the OLS line passes right through $(\bar{x}, \bar{y}) = (4.5, 74.375)$.

Why do we square the residuals ($e^2$)?

Because raw errors sum to zero, we square each residual so positive and negative gaps cannot cancel out—giving a Sum of Squared Errors (SSE) of $2.1548$ and a Mean Squared Error (MSE) of $0.2693$.

Section 1.5

Do Residuals Follow a Normal Distribution? (8-Point Diagnostic)

Reader Question 7

“Now in your response you said that the residual follow a normal distribution. I do not believe you. Show me now with visualization with our dataset value—give me the graph interactive like before and one by one.”

With only 8 data points, a histogram will never look like a textbook smooth bell curve. Instead, statisticians inspect three diagnostic plots one by one on our residuals [-0.08, -0.45, +0.18, -0.19, +0.44, +1.07, -0.30, -0.67] (Shapiro-Wilk normality test: $W = 0.94, p = 0.62 > 0.05$):

Important nuance: Linear regression does not force residuals to be normal—OLS finds the best-fit line regardless. Assuming the underlying noise $\epsilon$ is normal is what validates confidence intervals and $p$-values (and why binary $0/1$ outcomes break linear regression, since $0/1$ residuals can never look like a bell curve).

Section 1.6

Scaling Up to 300 Data Points (The Central Limit Theorem)

Reader Question 8

“So if we take more data points then it will be really the normal distribution? Short answer.”

Yes—as long as the underlying real-world noise is random and comes from many small, independent factors (thanks to the Central Limit Theorem). With 300 or 1,000 students, the histogram of residuals smooths out into a classic bell curve—unless the true relationship is curved (non-linear) or the outcomes are binary ($0$ and $1$).

Reader Question 9

“Now write a code for linear regression with 300 data points: (1) plot the data points, (2) plot the line using calculated $m$ and $b$, (3) calculate residuals and plot the points, histogram, and normal curve. Make it intuitive and show it here like before.”

linear_regression_300.py 
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy.stats import norm

# 1. Generate 300 synthetic data points (Signal + Normal Noise)
np.random.seed(42)
n = 300
x = np.random.uniform(1, 10, n)
true_m, true_b = 6.37, 45.71
noise = np.random.normal(loc=0, scale=4.0, size=n)
y = true_m * x + true_b + noise

# 2. Calculate m and b from scratch using OLS formulas
x_mean = np.mean(x)
y_mean = np.mean(y)

m = np.sum((x - x_mean) * (y - y_mean)) / np.sum((x - x_mean) ** 2)
b = y_mean - m * x_mean

# Predictions & Residuals
y_hat = m * x + b
residuals = y - y_hat

print(f"Calculated m = {m:.4f}, b = {b:.4f}")
print(f"Residuals Mean = {np.mean(residuals):.4f}, Std = {np.std(residuals):.4f}")

# 3. Create the 3-part visualization
fig, axes = plt.subplots(2, 2, figsize=(13, 9), dpi=120)

# --- Part 1: Raw Data Points ---
axes[0, 0].scatter(x, y, color="#3b82f6", alpha=0.6, edgecolors="none", s=25)
axes[0, 0].set_title("Part 1: Raw Data Points (n = 300)", fontweight="bold")
axes[0, 0].set_xlabel("Hours Studied (x)")
axes[0, 0].set_ylabel("Exam Score (y)")
axes[0, 0].grid(True, alpha=0.25)

# --- Part 2: Fitted Line using calculated m and b ---
axes[0, 1].scatter(x, y, color="#3b82f6", alpha=0.45, edgecolors="none", s=25, label="Data Points")
x_line = np.linspace(x.min(), x.max(), 100)
axes[0, 1].plot(x_line, m * x_line + b, color="#dc2626", linewidth=2.5,
                label=f"Fitted Line: ŷ = {m:.2f}x + {b:.2f}")
axes[0, 1].set_title("Part 2: Linear Regression Fit (m and b)", fontweight="bold")
axes[0, 1].set_xlabel("Hours Studied (x)")
axes[0, 1].set_ylabel("Exam Score (y)")
axes[0, 1].legend()
axes[0, 1].grid(True, alpha=0.25)

# --- Part 3a: Residuals Scatter Plot ---
axes[1, 0].scatter(x, residuals, color="#10b981", alpha=0.6, edgecolors="none", s=25)
axes[1, 0].axhline(0, color="#1f2937", linestyle="--", linewidth=1.5, label="Zero Error Line")
axes[1, 0].set_title("Part 3a: Residuals Scatter (e = y - ŷ)", fontweight="bold")
axes[1, 0].set_xlabel("Hours Studied (x)")
axes[1, 0].set_ylabel("Residual (e)")
axes[1, 0].legend()
axes[1, 0].grid(True, alpha=0.25)

# --- Part 3b: Residuals Histogram + Normal Bell Curve ---
mu, std = np.mean(residuals), np.std(residuals)
axes[1, 1].hist(residuals, bins=22, density=True, color="#8b5cf6", alpha=0.55,
                edgecolor="white", label="Residuals Histogram")
r_grid = np.linspace(residuals.min() - 1, residuals.max() + 1, 200)
axes[1, 1].plot(r_grid, norm.pdf(r_grid, mu, std), color="#dc2626", linewidth=2.5,
                label=f"Normal Curve (μ={mu:.2f}, σ={std:.2f})")
axes[1, 1].axvline(0, color="#1f2937", linestyle="--", linewidth=1.2)
axes[1, 1].set_title("Part 3b: Residuals Histogram & Normal Curve", fontweight="bold")
axes[1, 1].set_xlabel("Residual Value (e)")
axes[1, 1].set_ylabel("Density")
axes[1, 1].legend()
axes[1, 1].grid(True, alpha=0.25)

plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()
Section 1.7

Why Do Residuals Form a Bell Curve? (Middle vs. Ends)

Reader Question 10

“So why do the residuals follow a normal distribution? I mean the middle data points have more residuals than the two ends. Why?”

That observation gets right to the heart of statistics. There are two distinct phenomena happening here—one along the horizontal axis of the bell curve ($e = 0$ vs. extreme errors), and one along the study-hours axis ($x$ in the middle vs. $x$ at the ends):

1. Why the Middle of the Bell Curve ($e \approx 0$) Has Way More Points

Real-world exam noise is the sum of dozens of tiny, independent random factors: how well you slept, whether a lucky topic showed up, breakfast, room temperature, or misreading a word.

Imagine flipping 10 independent coins, where Heads = $+1$ point and Tails = $-1$ point:

  • To land right in the middle at $e = 0$ (5 Heads, 5 Tails), there are 252 different combinations—so random ups and downs cancel out most of the time.
  • To land at the far right tail at $e = +10$ (all 10 Heads in a row), there is only 1 single combination out of $1,024$ ($0.098\%$).

Exact combinatorics of 10 independent $\pm 1$ random factors ($\binom{10}{k}$ ways). Hover over any bar to see how many ways that net error can occur.

2. What About the Middle of the Line ($\bar{x} = 5.5$) vs. the Two Ends ($x = 1$ or $10$)?

If you look along the fitted line from $x = 1$ hour to $x = 10$ hours:

  • True noise ($\epsilon$) has constant spread everywhere: Whether a student studies 1 hour or 10 hours, the vertical cloud around the line has the same thickness ($\sigma \approx 4$).
  • Measured residuals ($e = y - \hat{y}$) are actually slightly smaller at the ends ($x = 1$ and $x = 10$): Extreme $x$-values act like the ends of a seesaw (high leverage). A noisy point at $x = 10$ pulls the regression line toward itself, shrinking its own measured residual slightly compared to points sitting near the pivot point in the middle ($\bar{x}$).
